
Methanol Utilization in Diesel and Compression-Ignition Engines: A Systematic Literature Review of Performance, Combustion and EmissionsSystematic Literature Review — Stage 12 Full ManuscriptAbstractMethanol is increasingly investigated as an alternative or supplementary fuel for compression-ignition engines because it can be introduced through blending, dual-fuel operation, fumigation, or dedicated injection strategies. This systematic literature review synthesizes research on methanol utilization in diesel and compression-ignition engines, focusing on engine performance, combustion characteristics, exhaust emissions, and fuel/injection strategies. The review was structured using a PICOC framework and based on a Scopus-derived RIS dataset supplied for the review. The dataset contained 348 records and no duplicate DOI or title groups were identified during the database cleaning stage. Following title/abstract screening using the defined eligibility criteria, 90 studies were retained for the current evidence synthesis. The included literature comprises experimental investigations, computational studies, and combined experimental–modelling research. The dominant themes are methanol–diesel blending and dual-fuel operation, engine performance, combustion behaviour, emissions, and injection strategy. A smaller but important subgroup addresses marine engines. The synthesis indicates that methanol effects cannot be interpreted independently of substitution level, fuel properties, injection method, engine load, speed, and combustion control strategy. The heterogeneous nature of the evidence limits direct quantitative pooling across studies. Future research should therefore prioritize standardized reporting, integrated performance–emissions assessment, validation of computational models, and longer-duration testing, particularly for marine applications.Keywordsmethanol; diesel engine; compression-ignition engine; dual-fuel combustion; engine performance; combustion characteristics; exhaust emissions; marine engine1. IntroductionThe decarbonization of internal-combustion engines has increased interest in alternative fuels that can reduce fossil-carbon dependence while remaining compatible with existing propulsion technologies. Methanol is one such candidate. It can be produced from several feedstocks and can be introduced into compression-ignition engines using a range of fuel-system configurations. Rather than representing a single technology, methanol utilization in diesel engines therefore constitutes a family of combustion strategies.The technical challenge arises from the difference between methanol and conventional diesel fuel. Methanol has substantially different physicochemical and combustion properties, including oxygen content, volatility, heating value, ignition behaviour and molecular structure. These differences influence mixture preparation, ignition delay, combustion phasing and pollutant formation. Consequently, the outcome of methanol use depends not only on the amount of methanol supplied but also on how and when it is introduced into the engine.The literature contains studies of methanol–diesel blends, dual-fuel systems, fumigation, direct injection and advanced injection optimization. Experimental studies evaluate brake performance, fuel consumption, combustion pressure and exhaust emissions, while computational studies investigate spray development, combustion chemistry, injection parameters and optimization. Representative studies include investigations of biomethanol effects on diesel injectors, methanol spray and pollutant formation, methanol compression-ignition combustion and methanol–diesel dual-fuel performance.A systematic synthesis is necessary because individual studies frequently use different engine sizes, operating speeds, loads, substitution ratios and measurement methods. Directly comparing results without considering these factors can produce misleading conclusions. This review therefore maps the evidence, identifies dominant themes, evaluates methodological patterns and highlights research gaps.2. Review MethodologyThe review followed a structured systematic-literature-review workflow. The review question was organized using the PICOC framework: Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome and Context.2.1 PICOC FrameworkPopulation: diesel and compression-ignition engines.Intervention: methanol used as a fuel, methanol–diesel blend, dual-fuel component, fumigated fuel or methanol introduced through an alternative injection strategy.Comparison: conventional diesel or another reference fuel/system specified by the study.Outcomes: engine performance, fuel economy, combustion characteristics, exhaust emissions, injection/spray behaviour and related engine effects.Context: experimental and/or computational investigations involving diesel or compression-ignition engines.2.2 Research QuestionsRQ1. How does methanol utilization affect diesel/compression-ignition engine performance and fuel economy?RQ2. How does methanol influence combustion and injection characteristics?RQ3. What effects of methanol utilization are reported for exhaust emissions and pollutant formation?RQ4. Which methanol utilization strategies, blend ratios and operating conditions have been investigated, and what research gaps remain?2.3 Search StrategyThe review used a Scopus-oriented search concept combining methanol terms with diesel/compression-ignition engine terms and engine performance, combustion and emission terms. The accepted search concept included methanol, methyl alcohol, methanol fuel, methanol blends, methanol–diesel systems, dual-fuel operation and fumigation, together with diesel engine and compression-ignition engine terminology.2.4 Eligibility CriteriaStudies were included when the title/abstract identified methanol as a fuel or fuel component in a diesel/compression-ignition engine context and addressed engine-related performance, combustion, emissions, injection or related operational behaviour. Studies were excluded when methanol was unrelated to engine fuel use, when the engine was exclusively spark-ignition, when methanol was only a reagent in biodiesel production, or when the application was outside the defined engine context.3. ResultsThe supplied RIS dataset contained 348 records. Database cleaning identified 348 unique records. Title/abstract screening using the current conservative screening rule retained 90 studies for evidence synthesis. The current evidence base should therefore be interpreted as a structured working corpus derived from the supplied Scopus export rather than as a claim about the complete global literature.3.1 Study CharacteristicsThe 90 included studies contain experimental, computational/modelling and combined approaches. Fifty-eight were classified as experimental in the current extraction, while 31 contained computational or modelling elements; categories may overlap where a study combines methods. The literature therefore reflects both empirical engine testing and increasing use of simulation and optimization.3.2 Thematic DistributionPerformance and fuel economy are the most widely represented outcome domain, appearing in 88 of the 90 included studies. Exhaust emissions or pollutant behaviour occur in 82 studies, while combustion characteristics occur in 78. Methanol–diesel blends or dual-fuel strategies occur in 73 studies, and injection-related strategies occur in 43. Computational/modelling approaches occur in 31 studies and 17 studies have an identifiable marine application.3.3 Methanol–Diesel Blends and Dual-Fuel OperationMethanol–diesel blending and dual-fuel operation form the central research stream. These approaches attempt to exploit methanol as an energy carrier while retaining diesel's ignition characteristics. The literature shows that fuel strategy is closely coupled to engine response. Differences in methanol fraction, mixing method, injection timing and engine load can change combustion phasing and emissions, making a universal performance effect difficult to establish.Representative investigations include experimental assessment of methanol–diesel dual-fuel performance and unburned methanol-related emissions, together with computational optimization of methanol/diesel direct-injection systems (Li et al., 2024; Huang et al., 2023; Li et al., 2024; Vishnoi et al., 2022; Hassan et al., 2025). The evidence supports treating methanol substitution ratio and delivery strategy as primary explanatory variables rather than secondary experimental details.3.4 Engine Performance and Fuel EconomyPerformance is the dominant outcome domain. Studies commonly report brake power, torque, brake thermal efficiency, fuel consumption or related efficiency indicators. Because methanol has a lower energy content per unit mass than diesel, comparisons based solely on volumetric or mass fuel consumption can be misleading unless the energy basis is stated. The literature consequently supports evaluating methanol systems using both conventional engine metrics and energy-normalized interpretation.A recurring methodological issue is the heterogeneity of test conditions. Engine speed, load, injection timing, compression ratio and methanol substitution can all influence the observed performance response. Cross-study synthesis should therefore distinguish between fuel-property effects and combustion-system effects.3.5 Combustion CharacteristicsCombustion research constitutes a major part of the evidence base. Investigations examine ignition delay, combustion duration, heat-release behaviour, cylinder pressure, spray development and injection parameters. Computational work has expanded this area by examining injection layouts, spray tilt, turbulence treatment and combustion optimization (Nkol et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2025; Li et al., 2024; Huang et al., 2023; Li et al., 2024).The key synthesis point is that methanol changes mixture formation and ignition behaviour. Consequently, injection strategy becomes especially important when methanol is used at high substitution levels or introduced directly. Advanced strategies may partially compensate for methanol's different ignition and energy characteristics, but their effectiveness is engine-specific.3.6 Exhaust EmissionsEmissions are investigated extensively, with 82 of the 90 studies addressing emissions or pollutant behaviour. Reported pollutants include conventional gaseous emissions and, in some studies, particulate or soot-related outcomes. Methanol can modify emissions through its oxygen content, combustion temperature, mixture preparation and altered fuel-bound chemistry. However, the direction and magnitude of changes are strongly dependent on engine operating conditions and fuel strategy (Orliński et al., 2022; Nkol et al., 2023; Li et al., 2024; Huang et al., 2023; Li et al., 2024).A critical implication is that emissions should not be interpreted as an intrinsic property of methanol alone. The same methanol fraction can behave differently when introduced through a premixed, fumigated, blended or direct-injection strategy. Future meta-analytical work should therefore stratify evidence by fuel-delivery method and engine operating regime.3.7 Injection and Fuel-System StrategiesInjection strategy is an increasingly important research theme. The literature includes studies of direct injection, spray characteristics, injection parameter optimization and injector behaviour. Research on injector coking is particularly relevant because alternative fuels must be assessed not only for short-term combustion performance but also for fuel-system reliability.The move toward advanced injection strategies suggests that future methanol research will increasingly combine fuel chemistry with engine-control optimization. This is especially important for high-load and transient operation where mixture preparation and ignition control become more difficult.3.8 Marine ApplicationsSeventeen studies were identified as having a marine application focus. Marine methanol research is strategically important because marine engines operate for long periods at high utilization and may have different load profiles and fuel-system requirements from laboratory-scale engines. The marine evidence includes work on high-speed marine dual-fuel engines and broader environmental assessments (Sun et al., 2023; Yu et al., 2024; Zhai et al., 2025; Long et al., 2024; Fan et al., 2026).The marine subgroup should not simply be treated as a direct extension of small-engine results. Long-duration operation, injector durability, fuel storage, safety, onboard fuel handling and real operating cycles require dedicated investigation. This represents an important opportunity for future research.4. DiscussionThe combined evidence indicates that methanol is best understood as a flexible fuel pathway rather than a single drop-in diesel replacement. Three interacting dimensions determine the observed response: fuel formulation, fuel-delivery strategy and engine operating condition. Studies that control only one of these dimensions may not be directly comparable with studies that change several simultaneously.The dominance of performance and emissions research is appropriate because these outcomes determine practical engine suitability. Nevertheless, the evidence suggests that combustion and injection behaviour provide the mechanistic link between fuel properties and final engine outcomes. A more integrated research framework should therefore connect fuel characterization, injection, combustion, performance and emissions within the same experimental design.Another important observation is the increasing role of modelling. Computational investigations can evaluate parameter combinations that would be costly to test experimentally and can provide insight into spray and combustion mechanisms. However, model conclusions are only as reliable as the chemistry, boundary conditions and validation data used. Experimental validation across multiple loads and fuel fractions remains essential.The evidence also highlights a distinction between laboratory feasibility and deployment readiness. Demonstrating that a methanol blend can operate a diesel engine is not equivalent to demonstrating long-term reliability, stable emissions compliance, fuel-system compatibility or suitability for marine duty cycles. Research should therefore progress from short-duration performance testing toward durability and real-duty-cycle evaluation.5. Research Gaps and Future DirectionsSeveral gaps emerge from the synthesis. First, experimental conditions are insufficiently standardized across studies, limiting direct comparison. Second, fuel-property reporting is inconsistent, even though density, viscosity, heating value, volatility and ignition characteristics influence engine behaviour. Third, performance and emissions are often reported independently rather than through integrated multi-objective assessment.Fourth, computational studies require broader experimental validation. Fifth, marine evidence remains smaller than the general diesel-engine literature. Sixth, long-term durability, injector deposits, lubrication effects and fuel-system compatibility require greater attention. Finally, transient and real-world operating conditions remain less consistently represented than steady-state laboratory tests.Future research should therefore adopt standardized reporting of engine specifications, methanol fraction, fuel properties, injection parameters, speed/load conditions and measurement uncertainty. Multi-objective optimization should evaluate efficiency and emissions together. Marine research should include long-duration operation and realistic duty cycles.6. LimitationsThe present synthesis is based on the supplied RIS export and title/abstract-level evidence. Full-text verification was not performed for every study. Consequently, detailed sample sizes, exact experimental conditions, quantitative effect sizes and some study-design classifications remain subject to full-text confirmation. The 90-study corpus is therefore a working evidence base for the manuscript rather than a definitive claim that all eligible global publications have been captured.7. ConclusionsThis systematic literature review identified a substantial body of research on methanol utilization in diesel and compression-ignition engines. From 348 supplied Scopus records, 90 studies were retained for the current evidence synthesis. Methanol–diesel blends and dual-fuel strategies dominate the literature, while performance, combustion and emissions represent the principal outcome domains. Computational modelling is increasingly used to complement experimental work, and marine applications form an important emerging subgroup.The central conclusion is that methanol performance cannot be evaluated independently of the engine and fuel-delivery system. Methanol fraction, injection strategy, operating condition and combustion control jointly determine engine response. Future studies should therefore move toward standardized, integrated and validated assessment, with particular emphasis on durability, transient operation and marine propulsion applications.ReferencesChen, H., He, J., Chen, Z., Geng, L.
